01 · The correction

You're right. Omission isn't dishonesty.

Your video-editing analogy is exactly the right test, and my earlier rule failed it. I'll restate the rule properly.

✓The actual rule

Never make a claim you can't back. That's it. It governs what you write, not what you leave out.

A case study that describes the problem, the constraints, the decisions and the craft — and says nothing about adoption — is a completely normal case study. Most of them are.

✕What I wrongly implied

That you should write "designed, not adopted" on the page. You shouldn't. Nobody asked, and volunteering it hands a reviewer a reason to stop reading before they've seen the work.

You never have to write the words I don't have metrics. Just don't write a metric.

The line worth holding is narrow: don't let a page imply something false. Showing YDPay screens under a heading like "now live in the app" would be a claim. Describing what you designed, and stopping there, is not. Same screens, same work, no exposure.

If it comes up in an interview — did this ship? — you answer then, in a conversation where you can give it context. That is a completely different situation from pre-emptively flagging it to a stranger skimming for a reason to say no.

The one exception, and it isn't about honesty

BookRion stays out. That's a rights question, not a disclosure question. You weren't granted showcase permission, and the work on their site now isn't yours. That's an exposure you can't write your way around, so it comes down. The brand identity stays — you have rights to that.

06 · Your correction

The design system isn't brand work.

Agreed, and it matters more than a filing decision — the two are genuinely different crafts, and a brand recruiter and a product recruiter want different halves.

Brand Identity page owns

The mark and lockups. The typographic voice. The colour truth — including the drift finding, where the stylesheet and the artwork disagreed and the artwork won. The guideline document. Applications: stationery, merch, environmental, certificates.

Design System page owns

Colour ramps and semantic tokens. Type scale. Spacing, grid, radius, elevation. Motion specification. Component inventory. Theming. And the contrast measurement, because that's where it did its work.

The handoff between them is clean: brand establishes what the colour is; the design system establishes what you're allowed to do with it. The contrast finding sits on the design system page because it's the moment brand truth became a build constraint — white on the brand blue fails, blue on navy passes, therefore navy-dominant. That decision governs both the website and the product, which is exactly why the page sits between them.

The three-way link you described

On /website — half-frame sectionpreview left · title, subtitle,
"View the design system" right
On /product — half-frame sectionidentical component,
same destination
/design-system — foot of page, two buttons← See it on the website · See it in the product →

One shared component, used three times. A reader can detour into the system and come back to either surface, or ignore it entirely and keep reading the case study. Nobody is forced through a token spec to reach the work.

On the Notion-style embed

I'd not embed the full case inline. Three reasons: each product should be its own indexable URL so you can send a recruiter a clean QUIV link; six full cases on one page is enormous to load; and a reader who wants QUIV shouldn't scroll through five others.

What gets you the same feel: on the suite page, each product gets a rich preview block — key art, the one-liner, its capability chips, a strip of 3–4 screens — then a link out. Visually it reads as embedded. Structurally it stays six pages.

And one scroll-scrubbed sequence, on QUIV only — the take-off action, frames driven by scroll position. Build it once, on the flagship. Six bespoke scroll interactions is weeks of work that will stall everything behind it.

Your own site already wrote the suite page's headline

"Six products, one workflow. Measure where your drawings live. Price from one shared library. Report from one place." That's better than anything I'd have written for the spine. Use it.

10 · The pattern

The sticky table of contents.

You're using it. Here's the spec — including an answer to your motion question.

Desktop ≥1100
Left rail, sticky, offset for the fixed nav. Sections, then a divider, then the sibling pages of the same project — so it navigates within the page and across the case study.
Below 1100
Collapses to a bar pinned to the bottom of the viewport; tapping expands the same list. Bottom, not top — thumb reach, and it doesn't fight the site nav.
Scroll-spy
Active item flips when a section crosses roughly a third of the viewport, not when it touches the top edge.
Progress
Hairline lime fill down the rail. Tells a skimmer how much is left before they decide whether to bail.
Lenis
The one that will bite. Anchor clicks must go through window.__lenis.scrollTo(target,{offset:-96}). Native scrollIntoView fights the smooth scroll and lands wrong.
Reduced motion
Not a design compromise — and not ours. prefers-reduced-motion is a setting the visitor turns on in their own OS, usually for vestibular disorders or migraine triggers. Only those people get instant jumps instead of smooth scroll. Everyone else gets the full treatment. Your site already honours it globally via MotionConfig reducedMotion="user" — this just extends the same standard to the TOC.
Accessibility
<nav aria-label="On this page">, aria-current on active, visible focus ring, real anchors so it works before JS runs.
Reuse
One shared component. Every long case study gets it — ADLM, NIQS, YDPay, Reni.
